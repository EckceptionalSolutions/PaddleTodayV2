- No matching production code change to the sitemap, robots policy, or route template occurred on August 18–19. This does not rule out an unlogged DNS, host, CDN, or origin incident; the exact start and duration of the documented August 25 Azure binding failure remain unknown.
- I compared the repository's August 14 route-template snapshot (`189ce67f`) with its August 21 state (`339c38ef`). It changed page layout and interactive sections, but not the route URL pattern, sitemap generator, robots policy, or route-link hierarchy. The river-only H1 and generic title/description formulas were already present in the August 14 snapshot and remained afterward. This makes a code-level navigation/sitemap regression an unlikely explanation for the onset; it also confirms that weak route differentiation was pre-existing rather than introduced by the redesign.

Current `listRivers()` returns 2,812 routes and `listRiverGroups()` returns 377 sitemap hubs, matching those sitemap components. Route-coverage expansion commits begin August 21 and continue through September. This growth happened after the traffic collapse, so it cannot explain its onset; it does mean Google's later discovery/indexing decisions were being made over a much larger and more programmatic route catalog.

In the September 20 Search Console snapshot, the **Alternate page with proper canonical** reason contained 29 submitted URLs. ## Interpretation

There is no current evidence for a broken sitemap, sitewide `noindex`, canonical-host error, or persistent crawl block. The sitemap is being read; the sampled former winner returns 200, is self-canonical and indexable, and Search Console showed it indexed after a successful October 2 crawl. Its October 5 weekly report still showed only five impressions and no clicks at average position 63.8. That is a visibility/ranking problem on a crawled, indexed page, not a sitemap-discovery failure.

The Search Console indexing totals remain stale: the page report says it was last updated September 20. A route in the discovered bucket has a live sitemap entry and two HTML link paths despite Google showing no referring page and no crawl; several crawled-not-indexed examples are technically sound. The sample points away from a current sitemap, canonical, or internal-link failure and toward Google’s indexing selection for a large templated catalog. The five-page sample is not a complete audit of 2,141 discovered and 298 crawled URLs.

The strongest timing signal remains the [August 18–21, 2026 Google spam update](https://status.search.google.com/incidents/LEubPCm2octf2uMqCFKE), which overlaps the abrupt August 19 loss. The update is not a diagnosis by itself; Google's [traffic-drop guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/debugging-search-traffic-drops) says algorithmic updates can shift positions and that a correlation does not by itself mean the content is fundamentally wrong. The link report also shows almost no relevant editorial references, while the site publishes 2,812 route pages on a shared template. Current route summaries are not universally empty or duplicated: in the 2,812-route public catalog, none lack a summary or source links; median summary length is 26 words; only 17 pages fall into eight duplicate-summary groups; and no route with a summary under 20 words also falls below both three evidence notes and two access points. A post-correction scan also found no public route whose summary says “planning-only” while its current policy supports live scoring. This weakens the claim that the entire catalog is mechanically thin, but it does not show that every page offers enough distinct search value. A sitewide quality/authority reassessment remains the leading explanation for the broad ranking loss and conservative indexing; this is an inference, not a proven penalty or update attribution.

Search Console's September 27–October 3 weekly comparison also fell during Google's September 24 spam update rollout, which the official dashboard said could take up to two weeks. That update began after the August collapse, so it cannot explain the original August 19 break; it does make the latest zero-click/low-impression week a poor endpoint for judging whether recent content improvements are recovering visibility. Reassess with complete weeks after the rollout ends, while keeping the pre/post August comparison as the onset evidence.

The additional segmentation strengthens that inference: route winners across different states, queries, and both mobile/desktop all lost visibility; two high-loss routes are confirmed indexed, recently fetched, crawlable, and self-canonical; and there was no production release at the start of the decline. The strongest actionable content signal is the pre-drop template itself: titles omitted the reach and state, and routes on a river shared the same visible introduction and near-identical description formula. This was not a deploy-time regression; it was a sitewide weakness already present when the spam update began. Google describes scaled content abuse in terms of large volumes of unoriginal pages that provide little value, regardless of how they were produced, and recommends people-first content. That policy resemblance is a risk signal to address, not evidence that Google classified this site as spam or that its update caused this particular loss. [Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) and [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

The current discovered-not-indexed backlog has a separate timing signal. In the nonrandom 30-URL Search Console sample compared with the last reachable pre-drop route-source snapshot (August 14), all 30 exact route slugs were absent from that source snapshot. This does not establish the age or publication history of all 2,141 discovered URLs, but it shows that this sample consists of routes added after the pre-drop baseline. Catalog growth from 1,117 to 2,812 routes followed the traffic collapse; it can explain why many currently listed pages are still awaiting Google's selection, but cannot explain why former winners lost rankings first.

The strongest content defect found by version comparison is historical and systematic. The August 14 route template used the exact same title formula for every reach on a river (`<river> Paddle Conditions, Water Levels & Route Info | Paddle Today`), a route description formula that differed only by river/reach substitutions, and an identical scored-route introduction for all reaches on that river; every planning route also used the same generic introductory sentence. On September 20, the route template began including reach and state in the title and the authored route summary in the visible introduction. A bespoke Little Miami title and description followed September 30. These are real improvements now present in production, but they postdate the August 19 collapse. The production template still uses the river name alone for the main `<h1>` on almost every route, with the reach in a separate paragraph, and most descriptions are still generated from the same generic formula. The local source-only change now uses each route's authored summary in the description and places the reach inside the `<h1>` while keeping it visually subordinate. On the current 2,812-route catalog, `river.name: river.reach` yields 2,812 distinct headings with no duplicate groups. Neither local change is deployed. This templated pre-drop presentation is a plausible quality/index-selection contributor, especially across sibling route pages, but it does not establish that the spam update targeted Paddle Today.

The About-page mismatch in an earlier draft of this note was incorrect. This checkout is on `codex/web-trip-rollout` at `49d4518c`, behind local `main` at `3348e0cf`. The stale branch copy describes limited Midwest coverage, but current `main` contains the September 28 correction (`2bc45d88`), and the live `/about/` matches that production copy: it describes contiguous-48-state coverage, separates scored from planning-only routes, and explains proxy limits and route source links. I reconciled the local About file to `main`; this was branch drift, not a production trust defect.

There was a real but later technical regression: the September 17 asset-packaging change inserted an SPA `navigationFallback` to `/index.html`, causing absent river paths to return homepage HTML with HTTP 200. The repository-config audit missed the package-time mutation. PR 29 preserved routing configuration, audited the packaged config, and restored real 404 behavior; post-deployment checks passed on September 21. This defect could pollute later crawl/canonical reports, but it postdates the August 19 traffic collapse and is fixed. The current missing-route and unpublished-route probes return 404/noindex.

Former winner route pages use generic template meta descriptions on most routes even though their visible intros and source/access content are route-specific. A live check confirms Namekagon County K–Whispering Pines still uses the generic description; Little Miami Kelley–Milford is an explicit exception with a detailed, route-specific description already live. A catalog-wide calculation over 2,812 public routes produced 2,803 distinct descriptions, eight duplicate groups affecting 17 routes, and lengths between 105 and 155 characters for the generated descriptions; the preserved Little Miami exception is 188 characters. This improves snippet specificity; it is not evidence of a ranking fix.

## October 8 ranking-update timing and recovery priority

The Search Console comparison above shows the visibility cliff beginning on August 18: impressions fell 95.9% and clicks fell 97.5% in the following 47 days, with route pages driving most of the loss. Google's Search Status Dashboard records the [August 2026 spam update](https://status.search.google.com/incidents/LEubPCm2octf2uMqCFKE) as starting August 18 at 09:28 PDT, applying globally and to all languages, and completing August 21 at 01:49 PDT. This exact timing makes the spam update the strongest external lead found so far. It is a correlation, not proof that the update caused PaddleToday's loss.

The absence of a Search Console manual action does not rule out an automated ranking change. Google's [spam-update guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/spam-updates) says sites affected after such updates should review its spam policies, and that improvement after changes can take months as automated systems reassess compliance. The policies most relevant to PaddleToday's route catalog are [doorway abuse](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#doorway-abuse), which includes substantially similar pages nearer to search results than a browseable hierarchy, and [scaled content abuse](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#scaled-content-abuse), which concerns large volumes of unoriginal pages that provide little value. This does not establish that the site violates either policy: route pages also provide a live planning tool and route-specific access, gauge, hazard, logistics, and source information.

The response should therefore focus on page usefulness and distinctness, not sitemap repair or mass keyword edits. Continue consolidating endpoint combinations when they lack a separate, source-backed user purpose, while retaining routes with different reaches, hazards, gauge context, access rules, or official trip-guide status. For remaining standalone routes, check that a visitor landing directly from search gets a complete route plan, clear source attribution, and useful distinctions that are visible without first navigating to the hub. Keep route-specific firsthand validation and original planning guidance central; do not create copy at scale merely to increase word count.
